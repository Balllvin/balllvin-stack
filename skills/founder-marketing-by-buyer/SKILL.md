# Founder marketing by buyer

Pick the motion from the buyer and the product. Then do it by hand. Living founders first. Channel essays only as the frame for channel and product fit.

First ten: see founder-led first customers.
World, samples, scarcity lane: see founder selling from industrial history.
Every public line: see how to write.

Horowitz: channel is a function of product and target. Do not pick a channel because you dislike salespeople. If the buyer is a company: why buy, why you, why now. Andreessen: the first ten only count if they pull. Usage or sales breaking you is the test, not a launch spike.

Two movies. Do not mix them. Casado / Nicira: no category yet, high contract value, founder plus a systems engineer on the street, six months on the story. Horowitz / Chen / Stripe-Linear-Figma: an end user adopts, then sales. If the founder cannot sell it, nobody can, and you do not hire a firm to invent the category.

## Same everywhere

The founder does the first close and the first install. The first buyer already trusts you or already has the pain. Narrow who you sell to, then adjacent. Charge earlier than comfort. Throttle, do not blast. Word of mouth is a result. Press is a tactic, not the first motion.

Chen: use little channels (one group, one town, one set of ex-colleagues). Big channels are bid-up. Marketing multiplies product quality. A bad product multiplied is still bad. Do not get addicted to paid.

## Sequence

1. Be the customer, or sit next to one this week.
2. Smallest thing that completes their job.
3. Recruit one by one from people who already trust you.
4. Install it, or do the work, so the next feature is requested.
5. Charge something.
6. Name the lover precisely. Invite more like them, not the whole waitlist.
7. Ask lovers for intros. Relaunch the story in rooms they already read.
8. If you want paid: cap it. If paid is why anyone shows up, you do not have a product yet.
9. You are not creating a category until customers pull and can name the job without your slide. If the job replaces a workflow, sit on-site. The founder still does the first sales.

## Companies

Go to the office. Install it. Do not send a link. Collison: why you, why this, why now, then every request in the inbox.

Charge day one if you will ever charge. Daher: a free pilot is not a customer. He still sold in year six or seven. Leaving founder-led sales too early is the mistake. You need the feedback to debug.

If you are selling a new system of work, sit inside the customer until it works. Schmidt: implementation is the early marketing. A self-serve landing page will not create that category.

Do not dump a waitlist. Saarinen: invite about ten motivated individual contributors a week and rebuild for that cohort. Field: cold email people you already admire, buy coffee. Conversion on a broad investor tour was low.

Casado (Nicira): founder-led evangelical sales until the motion repeats. Do not hire a VP of Sales to invent it. Price from the sales model. A channel or OEM deal will not create a category for you.

Lutke, merchant software: build an audience in the builder community first. A pricing change can be the real launch.

Enterprise: inertia is the competitor. A reference from a place you already sold is the strongest tool. Land is not expand.

## Serious individuals

Do not launch. Onboard. Vohra did the first ~200 sessions, tested price in the room, then used "very disappointed if this vanished" as the only goal. Build for people who would be upset. Not for people who would shrug.

Zhao: hide the meta-tool. Sugar-coat the broccoli. Notes first, then work. A product launch site as the first public sale, then individuals who bring a company later.

Linear and Figma sit on this border. Individuals buy. Companies later pay. Keep the first cohort as craft, not a blast.

Field now tells people not to wait five years to charge.

## Marketplace / two-sided

Go to the city where the users are. Chesky: knock on host doors. Founders were the photographers. Do it by hand until it hurts, then hire. Ask what would make them tell everyone, not only how to make it better.

Chen: seed the hard side first. On most consumer marketplaces that is supply. Build one atomic network that can stand alone (a team, a city), then the next. Order he states: supply, demand, supply, supply, supply. A press splash of disjointed users is not a network.

Cereal was cash, not a go-to-market model. Do not use "revenue doubled in a week" as a Chesky quote. It is not on his tape.

## Paid subscription / indie

Build in public, seed a community with people who already have taste, then charge to keep it clean. Levels: a modest Slack fee was a spam filter. Sell the next product from the same personal channel.

Do not depend on press spikes for retention. Do not over-build login before checkout.

## Consumer (not a marketplace)

Treat like volume cheapness or time scarcity (see the industrial-history selling skill), plus the ignored-geography rule. A demo where they already live (Houston on a forum) only if that community *is* the first buyer.

## What they refused

Collison: sending a link and hoping.
Field: a 9–24 month perfect roadmap, starting broad.
Saarinen: dumping the waitlist, launching unpolished.
Vohra: launch-and-see, growing before the disappointment score.
Zhao: leading with "everyone can build apps."
Chesky: staying in Mountain View while users were in New York.
Levels: unpaid Slack after spam, press as retention.
Lutke: a high take-rate on the merchants they wanted.
Daher: free pilots as customers, leaving the sale at the first no.
Horowitz: picking another company's channel because you envy it.
Chen: scaling paid because it is easier than fixing the product.

## Do not

Invent quotes or waitlist numbers. Attribute Graham's laptop line to Patrick. Use weekly-revenue folklore. Treat an investor essay as the founder's voice. Run ads as the first motion. Hire sales to skip the first ten.
