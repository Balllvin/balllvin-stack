# Founder selling from industrial history

How dead and living industrial founders sold, as told on long-form founder-biography podcasts. Tag every claim. If it is the host's reading, say so. Do not put a biographer's line in the founder's mouth.

Pair with founder-led first customers for the first ten users. Run how-to-write on every public line.

## Pick a lane first

Do not mix these.

**Volume cheapness.** Rockefeller, Walton, Hershey, Bezos, Kroc. Better and cheaper, then spend the savings on getting it everywhere. The offer *is* the ad.

**Luxury scarcity.** Ferrari, Chanel couture. Desire dies if it is in stock. Wait even when you have inventory. Sell the world.

**Time scarcity.** Ortega / Zara. Not expensive. Gone next week. The customer teaches you what to make because you own the store.

Using Ferrari tactics on a nickel staple, or Walton tactics on a dream object, is a category error.

## Then answer these

1. Who is the ignored customer, and where do they already stand?
2. What is the one idea a stranger can repeat?
3. What problem or desire do you name before the product?
4. Can they try it so the ad is unnecessary?
5. Is money going into distribution, samples, or media you own, or into claims?
6. Are you selling a world or a spec sheet?
7. Does the founder still touch the words, the counter, or the experience?
8. What will you refuse: research-as-strategy, me-too SKUs, mixed messages, sales running product?
9. What boring process would astonish a customer if you told it? (Hopkins: the maker is too close.)

## How they found people

Go to the ignored geography or ignored buyer. Walton: towns of 6,000. Lauder: the salon chair, then the Saks charge plate. Hershey: mass America, not the luxury eater.

Be where they already are, then own the next channel. Disney kept TV rights so TV could birth the park. Chanel used actresses, then perfume.

A membership, charge account, or franchise is a defined customer. Not everyone.

Word of mouth is a design constraint, not a hope. Bezos writes it as the most powerful tool. Lauder named it Tell-a-Woman.

## How they talked

One sentence a stranger can repeat. 1,000 songs in your pocket. Everyday low price. Limited menu done perfectly. Merit and cheapness.

Start with the desire, never the widget.

Sell a world, not a SKU. Chanel: privileged characters in a legend. Disney: the show is the park. After Don Valentine: money follows the story.

Rename so they can say yes. Youth-Dew is bath oil. Disneyland is a TV show.

Repeat. People forget that they forget.

## Distribution beats claims

Default: pipes, wagons, stores, samples, a private fleet. Advertising second.

When they advertise, it is often a property they own. The Disneyland show. The Michelin Guide. A keynote. Race results.

Samples and one dramatic demonstration beat boasts. Hopkins: a good product is its own best salesman. Lauder moved money from ads to samples. Take the chance on the buyer.

Michelin: do not sell the tire. Make the country drivable.

## Founder stays on the sale

Lauder at the counter. Kroc every ounce to selling. Chanel in the fitting room. Jobs on every ad. Walton in other people's stores. Bezos writes the letters that are the strategy.

Do not fully outsource the story. Hired storyteller titles are not the founder in the room.

## Refuse

Jobs: market research as the starting point, and sales running product.
Walton: the industry's rules.
Ferrero and Hershey: me-too products.
Kroc, Jobs, Sol Price: a wide sloppy offer.
Ferrari: instant availability when desire is the product.
Michelin: selling the widget instead of the conditions.

## Do not

Invent quotes. Teach a host's interpretation as the founder's own words. Mix scarcity lanes. Spend on claims before a sample or a channel exists. Quote unverified lines (a numbered Chanel saying, a floor-spill story as if it were on tape, or "storyteller is the most powerful person" as a verified Jobs tape) as if they were settled.
